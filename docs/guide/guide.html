DeusEdix: the guide

DeusEdix is a map editor for Deus Ex and Deus Ex: Revision. It opens the original Revision maps in 3D, with the game's real models and textures, and lets you change them without UnrealEd:

  • move, rotate, scale, copy and delete objects and whole structures (a helipad, a building);
  • add any object of the game, blocks (walls, platforms) and ready-made pieces of other levels;
  • replace the texture of an object or a structure with your own picture;
  • change the properties that matter for a mission (locked doors, keypad codes, computer passwords, what a crate contains, what a guard does and carries...);
  • write the mission's story: dialogues, InfoLinks, events and actions, compiled for you;
  • make your own characters from the faces, bodies and clothes of the game's characters;
  • make new maps (an empty room or a copy of any map) and link them to each other and to the game's maps.

The game's files are never modified: existing maps are edited on a copy of your own, and what you make goes into a folder of DeusEdix's (Revision\DeusEdixMaps).

DeusEdix is a fan-made tool, not affiliated with the Deus Ex: Revision team, Eidos or Square Enix.

Start it

You need Deus Ex: Game of the Year Edition, from Steam or GOG. Deus Ex: Revision (Steam) is optional: it goes into the same steamapps\common\Deus Ex folder and lets you make maps for Revision too. Each project is made for one of the two games (New project > Game). The installer finds the folder; if it cannot, DeusEdix asks for it when it starts (or run deusedix-cli.exe --set-game "<Deus Ex folder>" in DeusEdix's folder). With a Steam copy, Play starts the game through Steam; a GOG copy starts by itself.

Start DeusEdix from the Start menu. The first time it prepares its workspace from your game (links and copies inside its own folder: nothing of the game is changed) and compiles its package, which takes a few seconds. The 3D view needs an internet connection (it loads three.js, the 3D engine, from a CDN).

DeusEdix's window needs Windows 10 or 11 with Microsoft Edge WebView2 (part of Windows; Microsoft's WebView2 Runtime installs it if it is missing). Without it, or under Wine or Proton (Linux, Steam Deck), DeusEdix opens the editor in your web browser instead; DeusEdix.exe --browser does the same on purpose. It closes by itself when you close its last page.

The start screen lets you create a new map, open an existing map, or take Dr. Hale's tutorial.

The first time you open a map the editor analyses it and takes what the 3D view needs from the game (from a few seconds to a minute); after that it opens at once.

Opening and closing maps

The editor starts on New Map / Open Map, with a card for Dr. Hale's tutorial and your eight most recent maps. New Map opens the existing empty-room/copy dialog. Open Map searches your maps, recent maps and original levels grouped by mission. You can search by filename, location or mission name. The list in the top bar remains a quick switcher:

  • Script-built maps: maps made from scripts (tools\mapkit\maps\), including new empty rooms. Your changes are kept next to the script and applied on every rebuild.
  • Mod maps: your project's copies of existing maps (maps\).
  • Original maps (you edit a copy): every Revision map. Changes are saved separately and built on a copy.

The first time you open a map the editor analyses it (from a few seconds to a minute); after that it opens at once.

Browse for a .dx file... imports a map into the project's maps folder. Check the copy name and press Import copy. Existing names are refused: choose another name to keep both maps. The original file and the game's folders stay untouched. A map without a build is marked Not built yet; pending edits/builds are marked Changed, not installed.

File > Close map returns to the start screen. If the map or Story has unsaved changes, choose Save changes, Discard or Cancel. Saving here stores your work; Apply in game and Rebuild are still separate operations. Settings > General > When the editor opens chooses Start screen (the default) or Last map. Clear recent clears the list and last-map memory.

Interactive tutorial

Help > Interactive tutorial opens an eight-room training wing run by Dr. Elias Hale. The route turns through an L-shaped loading bay and a horseshoe drill course, with smaller rooms for conversation, materials and reading. Observation glass is used in reception and security. Show me frames the lesson and selects its object. The panel notices completed exercises:

  1. Reception: fly around the editor; meet Hale behind the original training observation window.
  2. Stores: select and move a crate, then place a new crate on top of the Crate stockpile platform around the corner. Pulsing circles guide you from the Objects search to the crate and the drop zone.
  3. Door systems: connect the wall button to either leaf of the double door; both leaves open together.
  4. Drill hall: give the trooper a greeting animation in the horseshoe practice course.
  5. Lab office: write a dialogue for the scientist.
  6. Materials workshop: select the plain crate, open Textures > Edit, paint its skin and choose On this object only > Apply. Hale's smiling demonstration crate is beside it. Merely saving a picture in My textures does not complete this exercise.
  7. Film stage: frame The Last Cup, a guarded mug of coffee. Tools > Add a cutscene camera (or + Cutscene) records the first view. Move the playhead to about four seconds, move the camera or change its framing, and press K for another key. Play the complete preview, then connect the SCREENING button to the camera with Play the cutscene. The exercise checks for two distinct keys in the stage, a preview of the current shot and a working button rule.
  8. Field test and library: Apply in game in Story, Rebuild, then Play from here. Test the door, greeting, dialogue, painted crate and screening button; finish with the library's tips.

The map is generated locally from the script in tools/mapkit/tutorial/ and the installed game's assets. Reception, observation booths and door leaves reuse selected architecture from the local 00_Training, preserving scale and texture alignment. Original map files are never modified or bundled. The burgundy reception floor uses the engine's mirror flag; the browser's Lit preview does not reproduce that reflection, so use View in game to inspect it.

The advanced wing is optional and opens north of the library, for those who want more than the basics. Every basic step offers Advanced ›, and Advanced wing sits next to Finish; the panel counts the training and the wing apart and never moves from one to the other by itself:

  • Interrogation: a dialogue with JC's answers (choices), a consequence that opens the vault, and a story flag the informant remembers next time.
  • Demolition range: a timer that counts down, what happens when it runs out, and a keypad that defuses the bomb.
  • Ambush yard: a trigger zone that sends two attackers at JC.
  • Code lab: your first script (Tools > Scripts), wired to a button with an interaction, then tried live with the Console while the game runs.

Dr. Hale speaks his lines (his voice is in the editor and in the game). The speaker button in the head of his panel (Mute tutorial audio) silences his voice and the background music.

Exercise edits and custom dialogue are kept when the tutorial's stock text is updated. Start over on the last step clears the training edits and story and rebuilds the map. The first time the editor opens, Hale offers the course; it remains available in Help.

Lighting of the view

Like Lit and Unlit in Unity or Unreal, the list next to Help (or the L key) changes how the 3D view is lit:

Shadedthe editor's own soft light: everything is easy to see; good for building
Litthe game's lighting: the light and the shadows of every lamp, their colour, how far they reach, spotlights, the ambient light of each zone. What you see is close to what the game shows
Unlitthe textures only, without any light

In Lit:

  • Walls, floors and ceilings show the map's real lightmaps, with their shadows.
  • Objects and characters are lit vertex by vertex like the engine does: the side facing a lamp is bright, the other side dark. A lamp lights an object only if the object's centre can see it.
  • Neon signs, screens and other unlit surfaces stay at full brightness, as in the game.
  • A slider appears next to the list: it makes the whole view brighter or darker, to compare with your screen. It does not change the game.

Lit shows the lighting of the last rebuild: lamps you add, move or change are not shown until you press Rebuild. An object you add or move takes the lamps around its new place at once, but without their shadows until the rebuild.

UnrealEd's views: the same list (and View in the menu bar) also has UnrealEd's Wireframe (the outline of every surface, the textures hidden; the objects stay as they are), BSP cuts (the textured world with the edges of every BSP polygon on it: where the engine split the surfaces when it built the map) and Zones (every surface in the colour of its zone, with a legend: the rooms that zone portals and ZoneInfo actors make). They show the last rebuild; a map read by an older version of the editor shows its zones after one Rebuild. Choosing a lighting brings the textured world back.

The screen

The layout follows the familiar hierarchy, scene, inspector and asset browser arrangement:

  • Menus and toolbar: File, Edit, View, Map, Tools, Window, Settings and Help. The Windows app uses a native menu above the editor; the browser keeps these menus in the top bar. Save, Rebuild and Play remain beside the current map. Themes and Rounded variants are in Settings.
  • Hierarchy (left): every object of the level. The structures (helipad, buildings...) with their objects inside, then the other objects by kind: characters, lights, objects, doors, triggers, AI paths, blocks. Click a line to select it, double-click it (or press F) to go to it, the bin deletes it. The search box finds any object by name or kind. Bulbs identify lights, people identify NPCs, and cubes, doors and routes identify objects, movers and AI paths. NPC icons show their configured relation towards JC at map start: green for allies, grey for neutral characters and red for enemies. Class defaults and unsaved relation changes are included, and Undo updates the colour too. Hover over an icon for its meaning. Player starts have a flag and helicopters have their own icon. A single piece taken out of a structure has the piece icon, not the structure's.
  • Browser (bottom), the media library, its sections on the left. Game: Objects (every object of the game: your own characters first, then the most used, the ways in and out of a map, decorations, weapons, characters...), Models (every 3D model), Pieces (every object of the original levels made of brushes, whole: see Adding things), Sounds. Prefabs: the structures and nests you saved, reusable in every level of the project. Imported: My models and My sounds, what you brought in with Import... (or dropped on the editor). The Console tab next to it shows the running game's log (see Scripts and the Console). Click a tile, then a floor or a wall to place it, or drag it onto the 3D view; sounds are dragged (see Sounds). + Create adds a block, a hole, water, a zone portal, a sign or a cutscene camera; Characters... opens the Character Creator. Nothing assembled by hand is in the library: the structures of the map you are editing are in the Hierarchy.
  • Inspector (right): the selected object's transform and relevant properties in foldouts. Changes and Messages can be expanded when needed. Right-click an object for actions for its type: a character offers Dialogues and Animation Studio; a light offers Light settings. The padlock at the top keeps the Inspector on this object while you select others (to drag something onto its fields); press it again to follow the selection.
  • The 3D view's toolbar (top left of the view, like Unity's): Move, Rotate, Scale (W E R), grid snap (G) with its grid step and rotation step, and the bulb that shows or hides the icons of lights, AI paths and logic points (see Objects).
  • Panels: drag a panel tab to the left, right or bottom edge, or into the scene to float it. The panel menu also offers docking and hiding. Resize by dragging its edge. Window shows hidden panels and offers Default, Scene and Inspect layouts, plus Reset layout. The layout is remembered on this computer. Floating panels remain inside the editor window.

Visibility, selection and game activation

  • The eye hides an object only in the scene view. Its game behaviour is unchanged.
  • The lock prevents accidental selection and edits in the scene. You can still select its Hierarchy row to inspect it or unlock it. View > Show all objects and Unlock all objects recover these editor-only states, which are remembered per map on this computer.
  • Enabled in game controls whether an actor exists in the next playable Rebuild. An unchecked object remains in the editor with its properties, animation and dialogue configuration intact. Toggle it back on and rebuild to restore it. This change supports Undo and Save; structural BSP groups use the eye and lock, without an actor activation switch.

Authored interactions involving inactive actors are skipped with a build message. Original mission scripts may still expect their actors, so test the mission after disabling one. For existing .dx maps, the editable source is retained in maps/.authoring/ with its edits; keep that folder with the project when moving it. Build caches can be reconstructed from the retained source.

The viewport uses a coloured bulb for lights, a camera for cutscene cameras, a walking figure for AI path points, a speaker for sounds and distinct symbols for triggers and other logic objects.

In the Inspector, hold the X, Y or Z label and drag left or right to move along that axis. Each pixel changes the coordinate by 1 Unreal unit, even with grid snap on; hold Shift for 0.1 unit per pixel. The object moves in the view as you drag. Release to keep the move, Esc to cancel, or Ctrl+Z to undo the whole drag in one step. This also works for blocks and structures (their objects follow). Click the number to type an exact value.

Rotate (E) turns an object every way, as in Unity: the green ring turns it round, the red and blue rings tip it forward or onto its side (a book lying on a lectern, a tilted sign), the outer ring turns it as seen from the camera. The Inspector shows Rotation, Pitch and Roll; type them for exact values. Pieces, structures, blocks, characters, doors and groups all support three-axis rotation. Selection outlines follow the object's orientation rather than expanding along the map's axes. An NPC's AI may turn it again while playing; editor placement does not disable its behaviour.

Scale (R): drag the middle square to scale evenly, as in Unity: right or up makes it bigger, left or down smaller, 1% for every pixel the mouse moves (5% steps with grid snap). It never flips inside out, however far you drag. The coloured handles scale one axis (blocks and structures).

Grid snap (G, or the magnet in the 3D view's toolbar): moves go in steps of the grid step (units: a door is 64 wide), turns in steps of the rotation step (degrees). Type any number in the two boxes, or pick one with their arrow; they are the same as Settings > Editing.

Icons: the bulb in the 3D view's toolbar hides the symbols of lights, AI path points and logic points, to see the level as it is. Hidden icons cannot be clicked in the view: pick them in the Hierarchy.

Objects scale all together (in Deus Ex an object has a single size, and its collision follows). Objects whose name starts with UC are the editor's own (interactions, the map's UCBoot...): you can move them, but keep them in the same room.

Several objects at once

Ctrl+click or Shift+click adds an object to the selection (or takes it away) in the 3D view; in the Hierarchy, Ctrl+click adds one row and Shift+click picks every row in between. Ctrl+drag or Shift+drag in the 3D view selects every object inside the rectangle. The gizmo moves them together (W) and turns them about their middle (E). Delete, Ctrl+D, Alt+drag and End (drop to floor) act on the selection. Pieces are included in rectangle selection too. Objects, blocks and structures can be selected together; the panel lists them. If a protected member cannot be duplicated, the whole operation stops with a visible warning: it never silently copies only the lights. Un-nest to duplicate independent scenery, or use a reusable prefab. Ctrl+C / Ctrl+V can transfer several actors or blocks between maps; for mixed selections containing structures use Save as prefab instead. Alt+click away from a transform handle still selects a face.

Mirror X / Y / Z (Edit menu, UnrealEd's Transform > Mirror) mirrors the selection about its middle, across the game's X, Y or Z axis. Blocks take their mirror image (a ramp rises the other way; a block with corners of its own, from Edit vertices, has them mirrored); walls, floors, stairs and roofs you drew too (on X or Y: a drawing stands on its floor), and curved or spiral stairs turn the other way. Objects face the mirrored way and keep their up (a model cannot be drawn mirrored); pieces of the map move to their mirrored place as they are. Their animations follow them. One Ctrl+Z undoes it.

With UnrealEd: Edit > Copy as T3D (UnrealEd) puts the selection on the clipboard the way UnrealEd copies actors, so Edit > Paste in UnrealEd puts it at the same place: blocks as brushes (a hole subtracts, a detail block is semisolid), what you drew as its pieces (cut by the holes, as the Rebuild cuts them), objects with their properties. The other way, what you copy in UnrealEd (Edit > Copy) comes in with Ctrl+V (or Edit > Paste T3D (UnrealEd)): brushes become blocks with corners of their own, the other actors objects, at the places they had in UnrealEd's map. A concave brush, a sheet, the builder brush and movers are left out and named in Messages (split a concave brush in UnrealEd first). The pieces of the map are UnrealEd's own brushes: copy them there.

Drop to floor and Stick to surface also work on independent Pieces and multi-selections. They use the actual geometry, ignoring the selected objects themselves. If no reachable surface is found, the editor reports it without changing the object or adding an empty Undo step.

UnrealEd's tools

What UnrealEd has for brushes and surfaces, on the editor's blocks and on the map's faces:

  • Top, Front and Side views (View menu, off at first): panes at the right of the 3D view. The wheel zooms at the cursor, a right or middle drag moves the view, a click picks, dragging the selection moves it on the pane's plane with the grid. Cut leaves out what is between the pane and the 3D camera (above it, in the Top view), so rooms show from inside.
  • Edit vertices (V, the Edit menu or the block's panel): move a block's corners one by one, as UnrealEd's vertex editing; it stays a convex shape. Delete takes a corner away, Reset corners gives the block its box and shape again.
  • Tools > 2D Shape Editor: draw a profile (or start from a rectangle, an arch...), then Extruded or Revolved, solid or a hole: it becomes blocks, one convex piece each, in one nest.
  • Edit > Clip block: two clicks in a Top, Front or Side view draw the line that cuts the selected blocks in two. Intersect blocks and Deintersect blocks: the block picked last cuts the others, keeping what they share with it, or what is outside it.
  • Look in the panel of a face (Face selection, Q): UnrealEd's surface properties, Unlit, Translucent, Masked, Modulated, Two-sided, Mirror, Invisible, Fake backdrop, Special lit, No smooth. Select same texture picks every face of the map with that texture, Replace in whole map gives them all another one.
  • All properties, the last section of an object's panel: every property UnrealEd's Properties window shows for its class, applied at Rebuild after the editor's own.
  • Select all of this class (Edit menu, or right click on an object).
  • Measure (M): click two points on the surfaces for the distance between them.
  • Camera bookmarks: Ctrl+1 to Ctrl+9 keep the view, 1 to 9 go back to it.

Folders (nests): keep things together

Like a nest in Premiere: select some things (Ctrl+click, Ctrl+drag) and press Ctrl+G (or Edit > Nest, or right click > Create folder). They become one folder in the Hierarchy, at the top, and one thing in the 3D view: a click on any of them picks the whole folder, the gizmo moves and turns it all; a double click picks one thing inside it.

  • A new folder's name is ready to type: type it and press Enter, or click anywhere else to keep it (Esc keeps the old name). Later, F2, Enter or right click > Rename names it again.
  • The + at the top of the Hierarchy makes an empty Folder (inside the folder chosen, if any): drag things into it. An empty folder stays until you delete it (Del, or its bin).

Cubes, wedges, cylinders, cones, spheres and domes

The Hierarchy's + also makes a Cube, a Wedge (a ramp, high at its +X side), a Cylinder, a Cone, a Sphere or a Dome (half a sphere on a flat base), 128 units wide, where the 3D view looks (standing on the floor there) and inside the folder chosen. Move it (W), turn it any way (E), resize it (R, or Width, Depth and Height in the Inspector); Shape in the Inspector changes one into another.

The round ones work like UnrealEd's brush builders, with their settings in the Inspector:

  • Sides: how many sides round (3 to 64, 16 by default). 6 makes a hexagon, 8 an octagon.
  • Hollow: the thickness of the wall, 0 for solid. A hollow cylinder is a pipe or a tower, a hollow dome or sphere is a shell. The editor cuts it into convex pieces by itself.
  • Top % (cone): the radius at the top as a share of the bottom. 0 is a point, more a cut cone.
  • Align to side (cylinder, cone): a flat side facing +X instead of a corner.

Unlike UnrealEd's builders they stay blocks: resize the box and the shape stretches with it (an oval cylinder, an egg, a low dome), and you see the texture before the Rebuild.

Give it a texture like a wall: pick one in the Inspector's Texture field, or drag a texture thumbnail onto the block (or click the thumbnail, then the block). Until the next Rebuild the block shows its texture the way the game will map it; Rebuild puts it in the game. Kind makes it a detail block or a hole (a round hole with a cylinder); water, zone portals, moving blocks and signs are always boxes.

Dragging an object into or out of a Nest keeps its world position, rotation and scale. A drop hint names the destination; cycles, locked groups and unsupported structural moves are refused. One drop is one Undo step. A Nest containing lights or other fixed-size actors can move and rotate, but cannot be scaled as a whole.

  • Un-nest (Ctrl+Shift+G, right click, or the button in the Inspector) takes everything out again.
  • One thing at a time: drag it in the Hierarchy out of the nest (anywhere outside it), or onto another nest to move it there. Nests can hold other nests.
  • Delete on a nest deletes it with what it holds; Ctrl+D duplicates it as a new nest.
  • A nest is the editor's own way of keeping things together: the map itself does not change.

The structures of the map are nests too. Open one in the Hierarchy: its pieces (each brush of the script, each piece pasted from another level) and its objects are listed inside it.

  • Un-nest a structure (Ctrl+Shift+G, or the Un-nest button) to take it apart: its pieces become structures of their own, its objects become free. They show apart at once; Rebuild makes them separate structures of the map. What the map script describes under one comment stays together in a nest named after that comment, so a big structure comes apart one level at a time.
  • Drag one piece or one object out of a structure in the Hierarchy to take only that one out (right click > Take out of... does the same). On an object, the Inspector has take it out next to Part of.
  • Nothing jumps: what comes out stays exactly where it is now. Ctrl+Z puts it back.

Structures

A structure is a group of walls, lights and objects that belong together: the helipad, a building, a staircase. Click one of its walls, or pick it in the Hierarchy.

  • Move and rotate it with the gizmo or the numbers; its lights and objects follow.
  • Scale stretches it, one axis at a time if you want. Textures repeat over the bigger surface, unless you tick stretch textures.
  • Copy, Duplicate, Reset (put it back as it was), Delete.
  • Structures with doors or lifts can be moved and rotated; their native motion keys follow. Their size remains protected from scaling.
  • Un-nest takes it apart into its pieces and objects (see Nests above).

In existing maps, moving pieces of the map makes the rebuild recalculate geometry and lighting: check the result with View in game. Some original maps cannot be rebuilt (Wan Chai Street, for example): there you can only change objects, not the map's geometry.

Adding things

  • Objects (the browser at the bottom): search any object of the game ("crate", "barrel", "lamp", "guard"...), pick it, then click a floor or a wall to place it. Shift+click places more copies.
  • B adds a block: a wall or a platform with the texture you choose; tick solid to make it walkable.
  • Pieces (the browser): fences, chain-link, grates, railings, pipes, vents, cables, signs and neon, lamps, windows, ladders, beams and columns, wall and floor panels, shelves, awnings... In Deus Ex these are not objects but brushes (geometry) the level designers made: the editor takes them from the original levels, each one whole (the brushes that make it, nothing around it) and only once even if the game repeats it a hundred times. The tile says how often the game uses it and where. Click one, then a floor (it stands on it) or a wall (its front faces out): it becomes part of the map with Rebuild, like a pasted structure; move, turn and copy it as one. The first time, the Pieces tab reads the game's original maps to find them (Generate Pieces): several maps at once, a few minutes in all; after that only changed maps are read again.
  • Game doors and elevators are in Objects → Movers, under Doors or Elevators · two stops. Pieces contains static geometry; Movers contains native moving brushes. Choose a textured preview, place it in an opening and Rebuild. Doors are individual leaves/panels; use them in Play to open and close. Lifts start when the player steps on, wait four seconds at the other stop, then return. Leave room for their original travel, including space below a descending lift. Geometry, textures, movement and assigned sounds come from your selected installed game. Each placement has an independent tag; original locks, keys, mission events, return groups and external controllers are removed. Immediately after placement the Inspector identifies it as a DeusExMover, with activation, move/wait times and door locking. These settings are saved and support Undo and independent duplicates. After Rebuild, Edit mover… selects the native actor for its tags, interactions, Door sounds and Animate settings. Moving, turning, duplicating and Undo work as with a piece. A mover placed by an older version can be selected as a native actor after Rebuild. Multi-floor/script-controlled mechanisms and unsupported tilted movers are not listed as ready-made pieces. This does not cut a doorway or build an elevator shaft, buttons or navigation automatically. On upgrade, Refresh scan adds the mover library using existing reference caches where possible; its first pass also extracts missing mover previews locally. No game assets are shipped.
  • Prefabs (the browser): select a structure or a nest and choose Save as prefab… (in the Inspector, the right-click menu or the Prefabs tab). It is kept as it is now, built, and can be placed in any level of the project as an independent copy.
  • Walls, roads, floors and ceilings: draw them (see Drawing walls, roads, floors and ceilings below), or use + Block (B) with the game's wall and floor textures, or start from a room (New map).

Drawing walls, roads, floors and ceilings

Tools > Draw wall / Draw road / Draw floor / Draw ceiling (or the same buttons in + Create). Click points on the ground one after the other; Shift keeps the line at 45 degrees; the points snap to an 8-unit grid. Enter, a double click or a click on the first point again finishes; Backspace takes the last point away, Esc stops. As soon as a tool is chosen, a sliver of what it makes follows the cursor (a piece of wall as long as it is thick, a slice of road, a tile of floor or ceiling, the first step, a roof's eave): it is where the first click puts it.

  • Wall: a wall along the line, 160 high and 16 thick, with clean corners.
  • Road: the asphalt with a sidewalk and a curb on each side.
  • Floor: a slab over the shape you drew (any outline, also with corners going in).
  • Ceiling: the same slab at the top of walls of its Height: the ceiling of a room, with ceiling tiles underneath.

Select a drawn shape to change it in the Inspector: height, thickness, width, sidewalks and every texture. Move and turn it with the gizmo. Rebuild makes it part of the map.

Like a block, a drawn shape shows its textures before the Rebuild the way the game will map them, holes cut in it included. Every texture field has its picture: click it for the texture picker. Or take a texture (click its picture in an object's Textures, or drag it) and click a face of the drawing: the face's own setting changes (a floor's top or its edge, a step's tread or its riser).

Stairs and roofs from A to B

Tools > Draw stairs (or Stairs in + Create): click where the stairs start, then where they arrive. Click near the edge of an upper floor and the end snaps onto it. The steps share the height exactly, about 16 high and 32 deep as in Deus Ex, 128 wide. While drawing, [ and ] make them narrower or wider, , and . lower or higher steps.

In the Inspector:

Textures

The Textures section of the right panel lists the pictures of the selected object (one per skin) or structure (one per texture of its faces). Edit opens it in the Texture Editor; Import opens a picture of yours (PNG, JPG...) there; Export downloads the PNG for another paint program; Original goes back to the game's texture.

Choosing a texture: every texture field (a block's, a hole's, a drawn wall's...) has a small picture of its texture next to it. Click the picture: a grid opens with the textures of this map, All textures of the game (search by name) and every package. A click tries a texture at once, a double click (or Enter) keeps it, Esc closes. The pictures of a package are made from your game the first time you open it (a few seconds), then they are kept. You can still type a name in the field.

Faces and texture alignment

Alt+click a wall, floor or ceiling to select its face. Shift+Alt+click adds or removes faces. Edit > Face selection (Q, configurable in Settings) lets you select faces with plain clicks and add them with Shift. In fly mode, Q still moves down. The selected faces are highlighted and the right panel shows Surface. Selecting an object returns to its normal inspector.

Choose a loaded Texture and press Apply, or use Align:

AlignmentResult

One TileFit one complete texture to the source face's width and height

Cylinder sides must form one closed outline and use the same texture. Pan U/V adds whole texels to the offset. Scale U/V multiplies the tile size (2 is twice as large, 0.5 is half). Rotate 90°, Flip U and Flip V change its orientation. Select whole brush selects all its visible faces; a structure's Surfaces section can select the faces of all its brushes.

These operations affect the full source face, including pieces split by BSP cuts. They are stored in order: Save keeps them, Rebuild shows the result. Undo (Ctrl+Z) removes the last edit. Changing UVs also rebuilds lighting because the game's lightmaps use those coordinates. Changes are written to both the rendered surfaces and their source brushes, so later geometry rebuilds keep them. If the source brush or its face layout is replaced outside the editor, select the new faces again.

Shadow detail

In Surface > Shadows, choose Low, Normal or High. High makes finer shadows and uses more map memory; Low is coarser. Normal clears both special detail flags. A selected structure also has Shadows for the whole structure, which includes every face of its brushes.

Rebuild recalculates lighting. On an existing map, shadow or UV edits alone do not rebuild geometry or AI paths. Messages reports the lightmap sample count and shadow storage before and after the build. The size difference depends on the faces and lights in the map.

Interactions (when this happens, do that)

No Tag or Event to write: at the top of an object's Properties, its Interactions say in plain words what happens when something happens to it.

  • + When it...: what starts it. A door: it has opened, it starts closing, it is blown up. A button or any object JC uses: JC uses it. A keypad: the right code is typed, a wrong code is typed. A computer: JC chooses an option of it (write the text of the button on its screen and what the screen says after). A trigger zone: JC walks in. A laser: JC breaks its beam. An alarm: it sounds the alarm. A character: they are killed or knocked out. An item: JC picks it up. An object: it is destroyed. Most things: JC comes near (within a distance you choose).
  • + Then...: something to an object (click it): click it in the 3D view or in the Hierarchy, and the list shows only what that object can do:
  • doors: open, close, open or close, lock, unlock, blow up, give JC its key;
  • characters: attack JC (alone: their faction stays out of it), turn hostile, become friendly or neutral, stand still, wander about, follow JC, walk or run to a place (click it), leave or come back (only when JC does not see them);
  • lights: turn on, turn off, turn on or off;
  • cameras, turrets, alarms, lasers, sound effects: switch on, switch off, play it;
  • any object: vanish, appear again, an explosion there (damage and radius), a sound from it.

Or without an object: a message on the screen, a goal (the same name completes it later), a note in the DataVault, a flag (the Story's flags: an interaction can start a Story event), a sound (from an object or in JC's head), take JC to another map.

  • Every action has its after ... s: seconds from the moment the rule starts (the door opens, 2 s later the alarm sounds). once: only the first time.
  • 🔗 Connect to...: click another object, and the rule is made with what the two usually do together: a button or a keypad opens a door, a zone sends a guard at JC, a switch turns a light on or off. The object that can start something acts on the other; change what it does afterwards.
  • Each rule is also written as a sentence ("When JC uses button UCLButton: open door UCLDoor, turn off light UCLLamp, after 1 s the message ..."). Green arrows in the 3D view go from the object to what it acts on; the Properties of that object list the rules acting on it (click one to go to its object).
  • Far away or out of view: when the other end of an arrow is off the screen (or behind you), a green label on the edge of the view points towards it, with its name and distance (to light Light114 · 307 m, or from ... for an object acting on this one); click it and the camera goes there. An object in view but very far gets the label just above it. both next to an action frames the object and the one it acts on together. A rule that takes JC to another map shows takes JC to ... by the object.
  • Logic (top bar): every interaction of the map, grouped by object and editable there too, like an event sheet. + When the map starts is for what happens at once (a message, a goal, a guard that leaves).
  • Check lists rules whose objects were deleted, actions without an object, sounds not chosen, texts left empty, maps that do not exist.

Rebuild puts them in the game. The game's own Tag and Event (Connections (Tag / Event)) still work: an object with an Event of its own keeps triggering it as well.

Scripting tags

Select an object and open Inspector > Scripting tag. A scripting tag groups objects for new interactions in the current map. Several objects can share one tag; it is not a unique object ID, a faction or a Story flag.

Auto, Custom and Original

  • Auto follows the object's type or current state. Characters use neutral, friendly or enemy according to their attitude towards JC. Lights use the nearest major colour: red, orange, yellow, green, cyan, blue, purple, magenta or pink; very low colour saturation becomes white. Brightness and switching a light off do not change its colour tag.
  • Typing a tag makes it Custom. JOHN stays JOHN when you change the character's faction, attitude or appearance. Even typing the same value as the automatic tag makes it manual. Auto explicitly returns it to automatic behaviour.
  • Objects with an authored native tag start in Original mode, keeping that tag as their scripting label. Auto opts into automatic labels; Original, when available, restores the inherited label after an override.

Use 1-64 ASCII letters, digits or underscores, starting with a letter or underscore: JOHN_2, not John Smith. Matching ignores case but matches the whole tag: GREEN matches green, not greenish.

Target a tag or filter a group

In an object's Interactions, add a starting event, then choose + Then... > objects with a tag…. Enter the Tag and choose the action. A light action affects matching lights, not characters that happen to have the same tag. The match count is a preview of the current map; automatic tags can change before the action runs in the game.

For a local group, choose + Then... > a group within a radius…. Set Group to Lights, enter green in Tag, and choose the Radius and Center. An empty Tag accepts all tags. Refresh group captures the current matching objects. Saved members remain connected if moved; Include newcomers also adds matching objects in the zone when the action runs. The tag filter is checked at execution, and the radius reaches through walls: it does not detect room boundaries.

Try this with a green light, a red light and a button: use the button's JC uses it event, target green, choose the action that turns lights off, then Rebuild and test in the game. Only matching lights should switch off. Save and Rebuild after changing tags or their interactions.

Native tags and story safety

Scripting tag is separate from Engine tag (legacy), the native Event and the character's BindName. A scripting override does not rename these identities or reconnect the original campaign. This applies to every character, including Anna, not just newly placed NPCs. Do not change an engine tag merely to put an object in a new scripting group.

A tag selects recipients; a Story flag remembers what happened. Assigning JOHN does not make Anna an ally, kill her or rewrite her later appearances. Changing AI, alliances, dialogue or character outcomes can still affect the campaign. Use Mission graph to inspect the recorded dependencies before making those changes.

Factions

Every character belongs to a faction (UNATCO, Cop, triad_red for the Red Arrow, triad_lum for the Luminous Path, mj12...) and has relations: how they treat JC (Player) and each other faction when the map starts, friendly, neutral or hostile. Members of the same faction are always friends. A relation can be fixed: it never changes by itself. If it is not fixed, it works as in the game: attack them and they turn hostile.

  • A character's relations: select them; in Properties set Faction and edit the Relations list (faction, attitude, fixed). The field suggests every faction of the game and of the map.
  • The Factions panel (top bar) lists the factions of the map, how many characters each has (click the number to see and select them) and how they treat JC. Change a whole faction sets a relation for all its members at once, for example Red Arrow towards JC becomes hostile.
  • During the mission (after a dialogue, when a flag is set, when JC reaches a point) use the Story: the action A faction... turns a whole faction friendly, neutral or hostile towards JC or another faction; A character... joins a faction moves one character; the event a faction turns hostile to JC reacts when that happens (JC shot one of them, or the story turned them).
  • Only one character: A character... becomes hostile to JC (or attacks JC right now). Tick alone to keep the rest of their faction out of it: in the game, someone who attacks calls his friends for help, and characters treat neutrals as friends, so without alone the others may join the fight. With alone the character leaves their faction and does not call for help. If JC then fires a gun in front of the others, they may still react to the shooting, as in the game.

To place a character: Objects (the browser), search its name ("Red Arrow", "MJ12", "civilian", "dog"...), click the floor; then set its orders, faction and relations in Properties. To make a character of your own, see the next section.

Characters

The Character Creator (Characters in the top bar, or Characters... in the browser) makes characters of your own from the bodies, faces and clothes of the game's characters.

  1. + New, or Start from a character of the game: it takes their whole look, their behaviour and their weapons.
  2. Body: the kind of body (man in a long coat, man in a suit, man in a uniform, woman in a dress, heavy man, child...). The pieces it shares with the old one stay: a face, the trousers.
  3. Look: every piece of the body (Face, Top, Legs, Long coat, Glasses, Hat / helmet...) has the versions that exist in the game. The arrows go through them, the button with the number opens them all as pictures; under it, which character of the game wears it. Faces fit every body of the same kind (all the men, all the women), trousers every body with the same legs. Random face and Random clothes mix them for you.
  4. Behaviour: Civilian (goes about their business, runs away from fights), Soldier (guards, takes cover, fights back), Criminal (attacks when provoked). When placed: stands, walks around, sits, dances. Health (100 is normal).
  5. Faction and Towards JC (friendly, neutral, hostile, fixed), as in Factions above. To make a faction of your own (a new gang), give the same faction name to all its characters.
  6. Carries: up to four weapons or items (a weapon needs its ammo too).
  7. Save. The preview turns: drag it to turn it, the wheel zooms.

The character is now the first entry of Objects in the browser: click it and click a floor, as many times as you want (each copy can still change its own properties in the Inspector). Place in map does the same from the Creator. If you change a saved character and save it again, its copies already placed in the open map change too (save the map and Rebuild).

Characters are files in tools\mapkit\characters\ (one .json each): Export saves one anywhere, to share it; Import loads one made by someone else. Duplicate makes a variant.

In the game they keep exactly the look you gave them (the HDTP models do not replace it), and their corpse wears the same clothes, also after JC carries it and puts it down. They have the walk, eye height and pain sounds of the body's original character (a woman's body has a woman's voice). They say nothing by themselves: give them dialogues in the Story (they have their own conversation name, UCEd_<their name>).

The first time, the Creator reads the characters of the game (a few seconds). Characters need DeusEdix's classes UCCustomCivilian, UCCustomSoldier, UCCustomThug: they are part of its package, so Install puts them in the game together with the map.

Animation: any object, doors and elevators too

Select an object (a model, a decoration, a light, a door, an elevator, a block) and Animate...: the Animation panel opens at the bottom, like Premiere's Motion or Unity's Animation window. It has rows for Position, Rotation and Scale, with their values at the playhead and their keys on the timeline.

  • Move the playhead (click or drag on the timeline), then move, turn or scale the object with the gizmo (W, E, R) or type its values: every change is a key at that time (the first one also puts a key at 0, where it rests). ◆ adds or takes away a key of that row; drag a key to move it in time; click a key to choose its curve (linear, smooth, ease in, ease out, hold) or to delete it.
  • ▶ plays it (the gizmo hides while it plays), ◀ plays it backwards, ■ goes back to the start. Once, Loop or Back and forth; at the start: it plays when the map starts.
  • Positions are offsets from where the object stands, so moving the object moves its whole animation. Rotation in degrees: 360 is a whole turn (two keys 0 and 360: it spins once).
  • Doors, elevators and moving blocks are the engine's movers: up to 8 keys, no scale, and whoever stands on them moves with them. A door you animate still opens when JC uses it, with your movement (a door that slides can swing on its hinge instead). A block becomes a moving block (Kind in its panel, or Animate... on it) and moves in the game as you animate it: a lift, a platform, a crusher.
  • In the Interactions: Then... → Play its animation, Play it backwards, Stop its animation, and When → its animation has finished to chain things. Each rule has a Timeline: its actions as bars at their time (an animation as long as it lasts); drag a bar to when it should happen.

Collision boxes

Objects > Triggers > Collision box: a box (purple in the 3D view; its Width, Depth and Height in its Properties). Cutscenes

A cutscene is a camera that flies along keys on a timeline, like the camera paths of a video editor or of the Minecraft film mods: the player watches, then gets JC back where he was.

  1. Look at the first view you want and press + Cutscene (bottom bar): a Cutscene camera is made, its first key is that view, and its timeline opens at the bottom with 🎥 Look through on (the 3D view becomes the game's screen: 16:9, with the black bars).
  2. Click the timeline further on (the red playhead), fly the camera there (right mouse + WASD, the mouse to turn) and press K (or ◆ Key). Each key keeps the camera's place, where it looks, its Roll (tilt) and its Zoom (field of view: smaller = closer). Do it for every key.
  3. ▶ (or Space) plays it in the editor: the picture as the game shows it, black bars, subtitles, fades. ■ goes back to the start; Esc stops, then stops looking through.
  4. The camera flies through the keys without slowing down at them (a curve in time, as in video editors): it starts gently at the first key and stops gently at the last. Click a key to change what it does there: Smooth (passes through), Stop here (slows down to it and starts again), Linear (straight to the next key at the same speed), Hold (stays until the next key, then jumps); its Zoom, Roll, Go there, Set to the view (the key becomes what you see now), Delete key. Drag a key along the timeline to change its time: keys far apart in space but close in time make the camera fast there.
  5. Path: Smooth through the keys (a curve that does not stop at each key) or Straight lines. Even speed spaces the keys so the camera keeps the same speed all along. Looks at: a character the camera always turns to (the keys still give its place, roll and zoom).
  6. ✂ New shot cuts at the playhead: the shot so far ends there and a new one starts with the view you have (fly elsewhere and press K for its first key). Between two shots the film jumps, no flight. Join with the shot before takes a cut away.
  7. Subtitles: + at the playhead, then Who (a name, in yellow), the line, how long (0: as long as the text needs), a sound or a voice. Drag them along the timeline.
  8. Events: + at the playhead, then click a door, a character, a light... in the 3D view or the Hierarchy: it happens at that second (the door opens at 3.2 s, the guard runs off). They are the Interactions When it starts of the cutscene (in its Properties, where you can add messages, sounds, goals): drag them along the timeline to change their second. When it has ended is in its Properties too (a guard attacks, JC goes to another map).
  9. Options: Fade in / out (seconds from and to black), black bars, can be skipped (a click or Space skips it: what was still to happen at once), only once, at the start of the map, JC visible where he stands (a stand-in of JC stays there while the camera flies), Length.

What plays it: an interaction Play the cutscene (select a collision box, 🔗 Connect to..., click the camera: When JC walks into it: play the cutscene), a button, a door, a character killed...; a Story event or dialogue (action Play a cutscene: under a flag, after a talk); or at the start of the map. A cutscene that starts during a conversation waits for its end.

In the game JC does not move, cannot be hurt and is not seen while it plays; the stories of the map and the collision boxes wait. At the end he is back where he was, looking where he looked, with his weapon. Check map warns about a cutscene with no keys or one nothing plays.

Models: every 3D model, and your own

The Models tab of the browser (next to Objects and Pieces) lists every 3D model the game can load, by package: Deus Ex's (DeusExDeco, DeusExCharacters, DeusExItems), HDTP's, Revision's, the packages of other mods (Packages...) and yours (DeusEdixModels). Search by name; a package shows its previews the first time it is opened (a few seconds). Click a model, then a floor or a wall: it is placed as an object of its own (DeusEdix's UCMeshDeco) with a collision round it, at the size the game uses it. Its Properties: Model (Replace…: click another one in the Models tab), name, solid, cannot be destroyed, strength, JC can push it, and the Interactions of any object.

Replace a model, keep the logic: select a mesh object, then Inspector > Model > Replace…. Click the replacement in Models (including imported models). This changes the mesh and its skins, not the actor's class or name: tags, links, AI, dialogue, inventory, transform, pivot and collision stay. The old per-object texture overrides are removed with the old model. Cancel or Esc leaves it unchanged; Ctrl+Z restores the entire replacement, including those textures. Rebuild applies it in game. The appearance is reapplied once after the initial HDTP setup; later gameplay effects remain controlled by the original class. Characters need compatible animation sequences and attachments; corpses and inventory/first-person models still follow the original class. Brush doors/elevators and map structures are not mesh actors, so they do not offer this command.

Import model... (Models tab) makes your own: a model made in Blender, 3ds Max, Maya... saved as glTF / GLB (best: its textures inside), FBX, OBJ (with its .mtl) or DAE. Choose the file together with its textures (Ctrl+click). The preview shows it next to JC (about 90 units tall); set its height in units (a door is 128), turn it, stand it up (a model made with Z up comes lying down), make it two-sided (leaves, cloth, thin panels). Import: Deus Ex's own importer makes it a model of DeusEdix's package DeusEdixModels (in the game at once when it is closed, else with Install), and it is ready to place. Up to 30000 triangles and 8 textures (made 256 colours, as the game's); transparent parts of a picture become see-through (masked). Your models are listed at the bottom of the Import window, where they can be deleted.

Animated models. A glTF/GLB, FBX or DAE with a skeleton (or shape keys) and animations keeps them. Deus Ex's models have no bones: every animation of the game is a series of frames of the model's points. The Import window lists the file's animations: tick the ones to keep and give each a name (the game's own names, such as Walk, Run or BreatheLight, are offered), choose how many frames a second they are kept at (15 by default; more is smoother and heavier) and which one the model loops in the map. Each animation is played and recorded frame by frame into the model, as a sequence of its own. A placed model plays its loop in the game (IdleAnim in its Properties, IdleRate for its speed); in the editor it shows its first frame. At most 4000 frames, and 3 million frames x points per model: for a long animation, fewer frames a second or a simpler model. A clip that walks away from its place (root motion) walks away in the game too: export it "in place" (Mixamo has the option). Some files cannot be used as they are: an FBX the browser's FBX reader cannot open (one with very many shape keys runs it out of memory) or one whose skeleton does not fit its model as read (a scaled armature, two skeletons: the Import window says so and leaves the animations out). Saving the model as glTF/GLB from Blender, with the armature's scale applied, solves both. A flat model (a rug, a panel) takes the height you give as the size of its longest side.

Simplify. Today's models often have 100 000 triangles or more; Deus Ex's own characters about 1000. When a model is too detailed for the game (more than 30000 triangles or 8191 points) the Import window says so and offers Simplify: give the triangles to keep (3000 by default; 2000-3000 suits a character, a few thousand a big object) and the model is made lighter where its shape changes least, its textures' layout counted too. It keeps a part of the model's own points, so the textures, the skeleton, the shape keys and the animations stay as they were. It also works on a model that is not too detailed, to make it lighter; Original goes back to it as it was read. (The simplifier is meshoptimizer, MIT licence, Arseny Kapoulkine.) A model made of thousands of separate small pieces, such as tufts of fur, keeps more points for its triangles.

Zones: water, rooms of their own, rain and snow, the sky

Deus Ex divides a map into zones: closed areas, each with its own light, sound, fog, gravity, damage, water. The editor makes them the way the game does.

  • + Water (bottom bar): click a floor: a pool 256 x 256, 96 deep, cut into it. Its surface is a zone portal, so the water is a zone of its own: JC swims and dives, the view turns blue under water. In its panel: the liquid (clean water, dirty water, toxic water that hurts with radiation, a burning liquid), where the surface is, a place name; untick cut a pool to fill a basin the map already has. Resize it with R or the numbers.
  • + Portal: click the floor in a doorway: an invisible sheet fills it exactly (it finds the walls and the top by itself). Put one in every opening of a room: the room becomes a zone of its own.
  • Objects > Zones: put one inside the closed area. Zone: its place name, ambient light and colour, sound of the zone (heard everywhere in it), hurts (damage per second, kind of damage: radiation, burned, shocked, poison gas...; a message), kills at once, own gravity, ground friction (0.2 = ice). Rain zone and Snow zone (Revision's): rain or snow falls round JC in the whole zone. Water zone, Sky zone for those who build them by hand.
  • The sky: a hole in a ceiling (+ Hole, click the ceiling) with open to the sky ticked shows the sky through it. A map with no sky of its own gets a sky box (Hong Kong at night, New York at night, stars, sunset, clouds, bright clouds, orange haze: choose it in the hole's panel).
  • Map > Sky & time of day: Day, Sunset or Night. It paints a sky of your own (its colours or stars), or uses a picture of yours or one of the game's skies, and sets the light: a sun shining in through every hole open to the sky, on the side of the sun, and the ambient light of the map. Deus Ex has no sun of its own: the sun is made of lights. Rebuild puts it in the game.
  • Clouds (same window), over any of those skies: None, Soft, Puffy, Streaks (long wisps along the wind) or Game's (the game's own cloud textures: the Hong Kong cloud bank, the wide one, the fluffy blue ones, the subtle one), and ↻ for other painted clouds of the same kind. Amount and Brightness (painted clouds), Size, Height (how high the layer is: its far edge, in degrees over the horizon; low clouds reach down to it), Wind (how fast they move; 0 = still) and Wind from (the direction, measured like the sun's). The preview shows them as the game will, moving: drag it to look round and up. They are made the way the game's own levels make them (Wan Chai Street): a sheet in the sky box whose texture the engine moves (Auto U Pan, at the speed the sky zone gives), so they work the same in the original Deus Ex and in Revision. The painted sky keeps the far clouds under the layer's edge. The clouds go in the sky box the editor makes, so a map that has a sky of its own (most of the game's maps) keeps its own clouds.

After Rebuild, Messages say if a zone is not closed (it leaks into the zone round it, often the whole map: its water, damage or fog would not be where you meant) or if the start of the map is inside a zone.

Packages of other mods

Packages... (bottom bar, next to Characters...) lists the packages (.u) in the game's folders that the editor does not load yet (Deus Ex\System, Revision\System, Revision\HDTP\System), and Another .u file takes one kept elsewhere. Tick them and Apply: the page loads again and their actors are in the palette. Decorations, characters, weapons and items with a 3D model go to their usual group, with the real model. Triggers, zones and other logic go under the package's name. They have their Properties and Interactions, and are placed, saved and rebuilt like the game's own.

A map that uses them needs the package in the game too: a package kept outside the game's folders must also be copied into Deus Ex\System (or Revision\System). Packages with only textures, sounds or models (no actor classes) add nothing to the palette.

Scripts and the Console

Tools > Scripts... writes UnrealScript inside the editor. A script is an invisible object placed in the map (it extends UCScript): Started runs when the map starts, Triggered when an interaction sets it off through its Tag, Repeat every few seconds. Helpers do the common things: the player, say a line, find an object, trigger a Tag, read and set flags, give an item. Compile builds the scripts' own package in a few seconds and lists the problems by line: click one to go there. Place in map puts the script in the level: give it a Tag in the Inspector and set it off with an Interaction like any object.

The Console (a tab next to the Browser, or Window > Console) shows the game's log while the game runs, as it is written: Scripts shows only the lines from scripts (log()) and from the Console, Problems only errors and warnings. During Play, the line at the bottom sends a command to the game's own console (summon, set, a cheat...).

Mods: export, install, play

When a campaign is finished, File > Export mod... saves it as one file (.dxmod) in Documents\DeusEdix Mods: its maps, its packages, a picture (the view you frame in the 3D view), its name, author and description, and the map it starts on. The editor refuses maps that need something the game does not have.

In the Projects window, Mods lists the mods installed in both games:

  • Install mod..., or drop a .dxmod (or a downloaded mod's .zip) on the window. Classic mods for Deus Ex or Revision are installed from their zip too (maps and packages; programs inside them are not run nor copied; a mod that replaces the game's own packages needs its own installer).
  • Play starts the game on the mod's first map, Starts on chooses another, Remove takes it away. A mod lives in its own folder (DeusEdixMods): the game's settings and other mods are not changed.

Map settings

Map in the top bar:

  • Music: Revision plays a track for every moment of the map: exploring (and the intro before it), combat, conversations, leaving the map, death. Same as the map takes the whole set of a map of the game; each moment can also be chosen on its own among every track of the game, and ▶ plays it. (The PS2 soundtrack option of Revision gets the same tracks, or the copied map's own PS2 set.)
  • Information: the name of the place (Place: the save list, the DataVault), the author, and up to four opening lines written on the screen when JC arrives ("Liberty Island, New York").
  • Ambient light: the light everything has where no lamp reaches, and its colour (Deus Ex maps use 0-20; maps with zones have their own in each zone).

Apply, then Rebuild. In the map they are its DeusExLevelInfo, its LevelInfo and a music actor of the editor's own (UCMusicInfo, a DXOggMusicInfo of Revision).

Player characters per map

Create and save a human character in Characters..., then choose Use as player to use its appearance in the open map. Alternatively, open Map settings > Player character and select it under This map. Apply, Save, then Rebuild. The Player Start inspector has a Change shortcut to the same setting; Player Start still chooses only where and in which direction the player appears.

For a campaign-wide choice, expand Project default, choose a character and press Set default. Rebuild each affected map. A map may inherit that default or override it with another character (including JC Denton). Thus map A can use one protagonist, map B another, and returning to A restores its chosen appearance. Map overrides use the map's normal Undo/Redo and Save; the project default is saved separately.

This first version changes the third-person body and eight skin slots only. Inventory, health, credits, skills, augmentations and story flags stay shared. First-person weapon hands, player voice, dialogue identity and mission scripts remain those of the game's normal player; changing appearance does not rewrite the campaign. Separate protagonist inventories are not available yet. All 17 human body templates in Character Creator are supported, including the different builds, uniforms, dresses and smaller bodies. Their gameplay collision and eye height remain those of the normal player: this is an appearance change, not a different-sized player or a different playable species. Imported arbitrary meshes and non-human bodies are not enabled by this feature.

Rebuild automatically adapts missing crouch-walk, weapon and other player motions from the installed game's player animations, including one-frame NPC placeholders. Existing usable animations, original frames and textures are preserved; the added motions are retargeted approximations, not newly authored bespoke animations. Footstep callbacks are retained. The copied body is stored in the private map, never over the game's original package. Preparation is cached; the first build of a body can take a few extra seconds. No game meshes or animation frames are bundled with DeusEdix.

Each configured map exposes an automatic Player is ... condition to the story tools (UCEdPlayer_<character-id>; hyphens become underscores). Only the current identity's flag is true. The choice survives normal map travel and native Save/Load in Original and Revision. Leaving configured project maps restores the original player's appearance. The editor prevents deletion of characters used by a project default or saved map; select a replacement first. A missing or unsupported character stops Rebuild with an explanation. Ordinary maps which have never enabled player characters stay unchanged.

Sounds

Objects > Sounds > Ambient sound: click where it should be heard (rain on a window, the hum of a machine room, wind on a roof, a crowd), then Choose... its sound in its Properties: the list has every sound of the game, the loops for places first; search by word (rain, hum, wind, water) and ▶ listens to it. Heard within is how far (the sphere in the 3D view), Volume, Pitch. Objects (a TV, a fridge, a generator) and lamps (the buzz of a neon) have the same Sound in their Properties.

Sound effects (once, when something happens), in Objects > Sounds:

  • Sound effect: when JC walks in: a zone (the purple cylinder, Radius and Height in its Properties): it plays when JC enters it. Only once, or again every time (Again after a few seconds).
  • Sound effect: when triggered: it plays when something triggers it. It gets a Tag of its own (SoundFX1...): put that Tag in the Event of a door (it plays when the door has finished opening), a button, a keypad (right code), a Trigger (Objects > Sounds > Trigger: a zone JC walks into), or use it from the Story. The Connections show the arrow.
  • After placing one, the sound list opens: choose its sound. In its Properties: Heard in JC's head (a radio, a voice: as loud wherever he is) or heard from its place within Heard within (the circles in the 3D view), Volume, Pitch. A sound effect that walks-in also triggers its own Event, like any trigger.

Doors have their sounds in their Properties (Door sounds): when it starts opening, when it is open, when it starts closing, when it is shut, and over and over while it moves; Heard within and Volume. The doors of the game have theirs already; a door copied from another map keeps them.

Sounds in the browser, and your own

Sounds and My sounds use tiles like the object browser. Click a tile to select it and inspect its duration, source, format, channels and sample rate. Play draws the real waveform and playback position in both the tile and Inspector. The Inspector also offers seeking, preview volume and loop preview; these controls do not change the sound in the game. Select a scene object to return to its properties. Then drag a sound:

  • onto the 3D view: a sound effect there, that plays when JC walks into its zone and is heard within its radius (both in its Properties, with the circles in the view);
  • onto a door or another object with sounds of its own: a menu asks which one (a door's opening, closing, moving sound; the hum of a lamp or a machine);
  • onto a sound in the Inspector: it replaces that one.

Your own sounds: Import... (top of the browser), or drop the files anywhere on the editor: WAV, MP3, OGG, FLAC. They are made mono (a sound heard from a place is mono in the game), 16 bit, 44.1 kHz, and become sounds of the editor's own package (UCSounds in DeusEdix, DeusEdixSounds in DeusEdix), which the game plays like its own; it is installed with your maps. Up to 10 minutes each. Importing the same name creates a numbered copy and keeps the previous sound. Drop one audio file on an object's sound field to import and assign it directly. Other file drops import into the browser, with a visible drop hint. A cancelled asset drag never leaves placement active. Download WAV in the imported sound's Inspector exports a usable file; dragging a prepared imported tile to the desktop also works in browsers that support file drag-out. Delete sound… in its Inspector removes it after confirmation (maps that use it lose it).

Check map

Check in the top bar lists what is wrong or missing, with go to:

  • errors: no Player start, a map exit to a map that does not exist, a character that patrols without a route, an object or a light inside a wall or a floor (as last rebuilt: stuck, invisible, or lighting nothing);
  • warnings: an event (or a keypad's, a security computer's) that triggers nothing, a patrol point leading to no point, patrol points with the same Tag, no light in the map, characters but no AI paths;
  • notes: a door whose key is not in this map (JC must bring it), unsaved changes.

What you added or changed is checked fully; the original objects of a map of the game only for what breaks the map (they have links the story and the game's scripts use).

AI paths and patrols

The characters of Deus Ex walk on a network of path nodes: they go from node to node along the links the editor of the game works out from them (UnrealEd's Paths Define). The map editor makes that network at every Rebuild.

  • AI paths in the top bar (or P) shows the network as the last rebuild made it: green lines are links both ways, yellow lines one way only. A place without lines is a place characters cannot find their way to (they can still walk there in a straight line when they see it).
  • To add paths: Objects > AI > Path node, click the floor where characters must be able to walk (along corridors, through doors, round corners: one every 300-500 units, each in sight of the next), then Rebuild. The other points of the same group: Patrol point, Wander point (wandering characters stop there and look at its Look at Tag), Hide point (where scared characters hide).
  • The points rest on the floor by themselves: Deus Ex makes no link from a point floating higher than about 20 units, so the rebuild puts every path point of your maps 15 units above the floor below it, as UnrealEd does. The original maps have their points higher (up to about 40 units): for the links the rebuild rests them on the floor too, then puts each back where it was. The links of an original map that the rebuild does not make again (Battery Park's subway stairs) are kept, as long as both their points are still where they were and nothing has been built across them.

Navigation (the route icon in the 3D view's toolbar, next to the icons button): the two layers of navigation, each shown or hidden by itself, ticked in its menu: AI paths (the nodes' network, P) and Walkable area (the navigation mesh), one, the other or both; then Test route and Fill AI paths. The same are in the View and Map menus.

Test route (the Navigation menu, or View > Test a route): click where a character starts, then where it must go. With the AI paths shown, the editor draws the way the game's AI would take, node to node, with its length; if no path node is near one of the two places it says so. With the walkable area alone, it draws the straight way on it. Click again for another test, Esc to close.

Walkable area (the Navigation menu, or View > Walkable area) shows where a man can walk in the map: the editor makes a navigation mesh of the map's floors (with Recast, the library many games use for this, which comes with the editor; it takes a second or two) and colours the floor JC can reach from where he starts:

  • green: near the AI paths, characters find their way there;
  • amber: walkable, but far from any path node: put one there if characters must go;
  • red: no path node reaches it: characters cannot find their way there.

Only the main network counts: path points the last rebuild left linked among themselves but not to the rest (a group apart) do not make their floor green, and the status bar says how many there are. Roofs and places JC cannot reach are not coloured; the status bar says how big they are. With the walkable area shown, Test route also draws the straight way on it (in the accent colour) next to the AI's node-to-node one, with its length: where the two are very different, the network makes the characters walk round. The walkable area knows the map's walls and floors, not the furniture and the other objects, so it can show a way through a crate or a barricade.

Fill AI paths (Map > Fill AI paths) puts the path nodes for you: one wherever JC can walk and no node of the network is near enough, about 450 units apart, each where a man can walk straight to the next one, away from the walls and outside the furniture. Where a group of points does not reach the main network, it adds nodes along the way between them (at the corners). They are ordinary path nodes, added as if placed by hand: Ctrl+Z takes them all away, and the next Rebuild (or Map > Rebuild AI paths) links them; the walkable area counts them at once. After the rebuild, run it again: it joins what the game left apart, and says which groups it cannot join (steep stairs or a ledge the game does not link): put a few nodes there by hand, closer together. On Battery Park it adds about 170 nodes and the floor goes from 23% to 97% green.

Patrol route: select a character; at the end of its Properties, Draw route, then click the floor where they walk, point after point, and press Enter (or Esc). They walk it in a loop: after the last point, back to the first. The route is drawn in orange with numbers; the dashed line is where they start from. Every point has a wait (seconds they stop there, turned the way the point faces: turn it with E) and ✕ takes it out of the route. + Add points goes on drawing, Clear route removes it. The character's Orders become Patrolling and its Order target the first point; each patrol point's Next point is the following one (all of this is also in their Properties, and in Connections).

Animations

What a character does when idle: in its Properties, Animation > When idle lists the animations of its model (breathing, idle gestures, sitting, cowering, dancing...) with their length; ▶ plays it on the character in the 3D view. In the game they do it while standing (Orders Standing) or while waiting at a patrol point, instead of breathing; Speed plays it slower or faster. In a conversation, in a fight or running away they are as the game makes them.

At a patrol point: select the point, Animation while waiting (the animations of the characters patrolling through it): they do it while they wait there. When the point had no wait, it gets one long enough for the animation.

Both work through DeusEdix's package: UCAnimSet objects in the map (made by the rebuild), so the package must be in the game (Install puts it there).

Pose here (Animation > Pose here... in a character's Properties, or right-click it): poses the character where it stands, against the map around it, instead of in the Studio's empty room: a hand on a railing or a desk, the back against a wall. The character shows its skeleton's handles: drag the hands (orange) and the feet (blue) onto the map and they stick to what is under the pointer (a hand lies on it, the palm against it, the fingers pointing on away from the shoulder; a foot stands on it); Alt moves them freely. A handle that sticks stays where it touches, and grabbing it again moves nothing until the pointer moves. A thin railing is easy to miss by a pixel: dragged near it, the hand gets onto it, and slipping just past its edge does not drop the hand onto the floor behind it. The hips (green) move the body (Shift: up and down) and, with Ctrl, the whole character moves in the map to come closer to what it leans on, the hands that hold on staying where they are. A handle turns red when the arm or the leg cannot reach. A hand that sticks closes by itself as on what it holds: round a railing or an edge (the surface ends just past the fingers), relaxed on a table top; the Grip sliders override it. Start from gives a ready pose, placed on what you click next: Railing (both hands on it), Elbows (leaning on it), Wall (the back against it), Table (both hands on the top) and Sit (on an edge, a crate, a bench, facing the camera): the character turns, comes close enough for its arms to reach (never into what it holds; it leans more when it cannot come closer) and puts its hands and feet down; a seat too high to sit on with the feet on the floor is said. The panel leans, turns and bends the upper body, closes the hands (Grip) and keeps the character breathing. Save makes it an animation of the Animation Studio (Pose<name>), puts it on the character's models and gives it to the character as what it does when idle, with Stays put (it never turns away from what it leans on); Rebuild puts it in the map. Opening Pose here again on that character goes back to the same pose. Animate in the Studio... saves the pose as a new animation with the name you give it, without changing the character, and opens it in the Animation Studio: the pose is its first key, to go on animating from there. Ctrl+Z and Ctrl+Y (or the arrows in the panel) step back and forth through the changes made while posing, the character's place in the map included; Reset (standing again) is one of them, so a mistake never means starting over. Esc or Cancel leaves without changing anything.

Animation Studio

Animations in the top bar (or Animation Studio... in a character's Properties) opens the Studio, to make new animations for the characters, change the game's, or bring in animations from other programs.

Deus Ex animates its characters frame by frame, without a skeleton. The Studio finds a skeleton in every model by itself, from the game's own animations: the parts of the body that always move together are a bone, the points where two of them turn are the joints. It takes a moment the first time a model is opened; Make the skeleton again redoes it.

Every human gets the same skeleton of 17 bones. The joints are placed where a body has them (shoulders, elbows, hips, knees and ankles at their usual share of the height) and then fitted to the model's own motion, so a long coat or a skirt does not move a knee; the knee sits on the line from the hip to the ankle, a little forward. Children are the same skeleton, smaller.

The game's characters never bend their wrists, so the hands are found from the model itself: the vertices of the lower arm with the skin's texture (the head's) are the hand, and the wrist is where the sleeve ends; with gloves or bare arms, what lies past the wrist. The animations made before the hands had bones (18 October 2026) keep the skeleton they were made with, so they look the same.

  • Character and Model: the character to work on, and its model. A soldier of the game can have two: the original and the HDTP one (Revision's high definition models). The Studio shows the one the game shows and puts every animation on both.
  • Game animations: click one to play it. Copy makes it an animation of yours, to change.
  • + New: an animation of your own, starting from the rest pose. Click a ball of the skeleton (or the body) to pick a bone and turn it with the rings; the hips also move (G or W move, R or E turn). Every change is a key at that frame (the yellow diamonds on the timeline); between keys the Studio moves smoothly. Go to another frame (the slider, the arrow keys), pose again. Space plays. Delete removes the key of the frame, Mirror swaps left and right, Reset bone puts a bone back, From game ... Pose copies the pose of a frame of a game animation. Frames and fps give its length, loops whether it repeats.
  • Import file: an animation made elsewhere, on any human skeleton: BVH (motion capture: free libraries such as CMU's), FBX (for example from Mixamo: download it in FBX, with or without skin) or glTF/GLB (Blender exports it). The Studio recognises the bones by their usual names (Hips, Spine, LeftArm, LeftForeArm, LeftUpLeg, upperarm_l, thigh_l...) and moves each bone of the Deus Ex skeleton the way the file's does, whatever the file's rest pose (a T-pose too) and size. It stays in place: walking forward is the game's job. The bones the file does not have follow their parent (they are listed). A file on the Studio's own skeleton (see Export .glb) comes back as it was made: the body's motion and the frame rate too.
  • IK (the buttons L hand, R hand, L foot, R foot under the frame's title): an arm or a leg moved by its hand or foot. A box at the hand or foot is the controller: G moves it and the limb follows, R turns the hand or foot; the diamond (the pole) is where the elbow or the knee points. The controller has keys of its own (its row in the list, Key controller), and a foot on IK stays where it is while the body moves. Clicking the button again goes back to turning the bones one by one (FK): the bones get keys holding the IK's poses.
  • Grip (in the panel when a hand, or an arm's IK, is selected): how far the hand is closed, from 0 (open) to 100. It is keyed on its own row (Left grip, Right grip) like a bone. The game's models have no fingers of their own, so the closing is the game's: the way the model's own animations close its hands (holding a rifle), so it looks as in the game. The HDTP models never close their hands in their animations: their fingers bend at the knuckles instead. Without grip keys the hands are as before.
  • Export .glb: the character with its skeleton, its weights, its textures and the animation open, for Blender or any other program that reads glTF. In Blender set the scene to the animation's frame rate (the Studio says it), then File > Import > glTF 2.0. Animate there, export glTF again and Import file here.
  • Graph editor: pick a bone and click its curves (or Edit curves) to adjust its X/Y/Z rotations in degrees. Ctrl+Space or the maximize button fills the Studio with it, and again gives the view back; it fits the curves when it opens. Body position edits the character's position (X forward, Y up, Z right). Opening the graph does not change an animation. Enabling curves retains its poses at every game frame, including imported motion; constant axes start with one key. The graph and the game bake use the same curves. Click or Shift-click keys, drag to change their frame/value, or type precise values. Smooth rounds the movement without overshooting the neighboring values, Linear maintains a constant speed, and Hold retains a value until the next key. Interpolation applies after the selected key. Add a key at the playhead, duplicate selected keys there, or delete them; collisions with other keys are rejected. Fit all/selected, mouse-wheel zoom and middle-button drag help navigate. Graph keys affect their selected channel; the yellow timeline keys remain whole poses.
  • Numbers: hold the label of a number (X, Y, Z, Scale %, Frames, fps) and drag left or right; a faster mouse makes bigger steps, Shift is fine, Esc puts it back. One drag is one undo step.
  • Keys as in Blender: G move, R rotate, S scale (props), Alt+G / Alt+R / Alt+S back to rest; I keys the bone (Shift+I the pose), Alt+I or X deletes the key; Up / Down the next and previous key, Shift+Left / Right the first and last frame; Ctrl+C / Ctrl+V copy and paste a pose (Ctrl+Shift+V mirrored); F or Numpad . looks at the selection; Numpad 1 / 3 / 7 front, right and top (Ctrl: the other side). In the Graph: A all keys, Alt+A none, G moves the keys with the mouse (a click or Enter places them, Esc or a right click puts them back), X delete, Shift+D duplicate, I a key at the playhead, T easing, Home fit, F fit the selected keys.
  • Undo / Redo: the Studio keeps its own history, including posing, curves and animation settings. Use the graph buttons or Ctrl+Z, Ctrl+Shift+Z / Ctrl+Y. One drag is one undo step.
  • Save puts it in the game: it becomes frames of the model, like the game's own, for every model of the character (original and HDTP), in DeusEdix's package DeusEdixAnims.u, installed in the game when it is closed.

To use it: a character's Properties, When idle, the group Animation Studio (yours). Every human character can use it: the Studio puts it on that character's models when you choose it (a few seconds). In the game the character gets a copy of its model with your animations added (all the game's are still there), whatever model the game gave it (HDTP or not).

Animations are files in tools\mapkit\anims\ (<name>.json: the keys of the bones), the skeletons in anims\rigs\, the frames made for each model in anims\baked\.

Props attached to bones

In Animation Studio, open Props and choose + Add prop. Search the game's objects (for example Liquor40oz for a bottle), select one and attach it. The selected bone is used; the default hips selection attaches to Right hand. Bone can move the attachment to any other bone.

  • Click the prop in the view to select it (a yellow box): the gizmo moves (G), turns (R) and scales (S) it in every direction; it stays in its bone's frame. Alt+G / Alt+R / Alt+S put its offset, turn or size back.
  • Offset and Rotation fit the grip in bone-local axes (X forward, Y up, Z right at rest). Scale % changes the object's size. Snap to bone resets its offset and rotation.
  • Enabled includes or excludes it in both the preview and game; Remove removes this attachment only. All prop edits support the Studio's Undo/Redo and stay with the saved animation.
  • Pose the arm normally and scrub or play: the prop follows the bone. Save, assign the animation to the character, then Rebuild the map to include the attachments. Saving alone does not rebuild maps.

Props are visual accessories, not pickups: no collision, inventory or AI changes. They appear only while the character plays that animation, follow its speed and loops, and disappear when it changes animation or dies. Up to eight props per animation, on Original and HDTP character models. This first version has a fixed grip throughout the animation: no keyed pickup/drop or hand switching yet. The game's Cigarettes object is a packet, not a single cigarette.

New map

New map (top bar, next to the list of maps) makes a map of your own:

  • Name and Mission: the file becomes <mission>_<name>.dx (for example 06_Warehouse_9). The map belongs to that part of the campaign: the mission's goals, flags and InfoLinks work in it. Place name is what the game shows as the location (optional).
  • An empty room to build in: its size (width, depth, height; a door is 64 x 128) and the textures of floor, walls and ceiling (smooth concrete by default). It has lights, the start of the map and an arrival point called Start. It is a map script (tools\mapkit\maps\new_<map>.py) built at once: add Pieces, Objects, characters and blocks, then Rebuild.
  • A copy of a map: any map of the game or of yours under the new name, as a map of your own (maps\): change it as you like, the original stays as it is.

Install puts the new map in the game.

Links between maps

JC leaves a map through a map exit: he walks into it and the next map loads. He appears at an arrival point of that map, or at its start (its PlayerStart). Both are in Objects > Level (with Player start), or in the Links panel.

  • Place a Map exit where it makes sense (a doorway, the end of an alley, the top of a stair). In its Properties, Leads to lists every map; arriving at lists the arrival points of the map you chose (the game's own ones too), or the start of the map.
  • Place an Arrival point where JC should appear, turned the way he should look. Its Tag is its name: other maps lead to <this map>#<Tag>.
  • Links (top bar) shows the ways out of this map (where each exit leads), its arrival points, and every map of the game or of yours with an exit that leads here.
  • A dialogue or an event of the Story can also take JC to another map (action Take JC to another map).

To put a new map in the campaign: open the map of the campaign JC should come from (for example 06_HongKong_WanChai_Market; the editor makes your copy, the original stays as it is). Place a map exit there, leading to your map at Start. In your map, place a map exit back to that map, at one of its arrival points (the list shows the game's own) or at a new arrival point you place there. Rebuild both maps and Install. Play from here lets you try the way in. The exits are read from the built maps: after a Rebuild, Links and the lists show them.

Mission graph (read only)

The map icon at the top of the Inspector, to the right of its padlock, opens Mission graph in a separate analysis window. A pinned Inspector uses the object displayed there. The analyser reads saved maps, characters, flags, conversations and scripts in the active project: Original, Revision and UNATCO are analysed separately, without mixing their game variants.

First inspection

  1. Select Anna or another character and open Mission graph. The default view is Rules.
  2. Choose Mission and Map. The mission starts from the selected map's number; All missions also shows later appearances. Rules are grouped by mission and map. Map not resolved means the scanner has not established a map, not that the rule runs everywhere.
  3. Expand a rule card. When, Conditions and Actions describe the source block. Reads flags are states that it checks; Writes flags are states that it changes. These are not current values from a running save. Separate branches stay separate; conditions retain AND, OR and negation.
  4. Click a flag. Who writes it and Who checks it list the scanned rules across all missions. Open an entry to see that rule, even if it concerns another character. Back to Anna Navarre (or your starting selection) restores the original subject and mission filter.
  5. Details → Source statements & conditions shows the file, line and individual source spans. Notes explain actor loops, inherited script bindings, partial control flow and unresolved names.

For a first Anna exercise, choose Mission 03, map 03_NYC_UNATCOHQ, and find MS_AnnaDeskHome. The block checks AnnaAtDesk and MS_AnnaDeskHome, sets Anna's home position and marks the latter flag true. Click MS_AnnaDeskHome to compare its readers and writers. Original and Revision may both have source definitions in a Revision project; this does not prove that both execute.

Rules match the selected subject by explicit class, dialogue identity or actor reference. A flag whose name starts with that identity is separately labelled Matching flag name · not an actor reference. Unrelated flags from other branches of the same script are not automatically treated as this character's rules. A script may select several instances of a class; a conversation owner is an identity, not a guaranteed instance in the selected map.

Who writes it and Who checks it name scripts, conversations or authored rules, not necessarily the character performing the action. No writer found does not mean that a flag is unused: the engine, missing source code and computed flag names may add accesses that cannot be resolved statically. Conversation events and choices are separate records; their branch order is not simulated.

Connections in the view selector keeps the previous graph available: Dialogues, Behaviour, Story states and Other appearances open filterable reference lists. Select a reference to see one path, then click an arrow for its explanation or expand Direct connections to continue. Clear path clears that path. Find… searches project-wide; Back still restores your first object. Other appearances are separate instances with a shared class or identity, not proof of a unique character across maps. Shared scripts may also concern other characters. Arrows are references, not execution order. Long lists use Show more.

Findings and coverage

Findings lists issues on the current graph selection/path, with an expandable project list:

  • Error: a verifiable saved-reference problem, such as a rule targeting an actor deleted from a fully decoded map.
  • Review: a possible problem that needs checking, such as a destination outside scanned maps.
  • Unverified: missing sources, incomplete maps, computed names or runtime actor selection.

More → Coverage reports what was scanned. Rule cards reconstruct literal access and lexical if/else conditions, not full execution. Function calls, inherited dispatch, jumps, returns and switch branches may need source review; partial control flow is marked. Map guards are explicit when found; script bindings are candidate scopes, and numbered mission grouping follows the source/map naming. Authored rule cards retain saved conditions/actions, including choices, without simulating them.

The graph is not a live debugger, a narrative consistency checker or a visual script editor. It cannot prove that a rule runs, that branches conflict, or that changing a character is safe. No errors means neither complete coverage nor no risk.

Refreshing the analysis

Refresh reads saved project files again; reopening can reuse a cached scan. Unsaved changes are not included. More → Read game sources explicitly reads installed packages into local analysis caches when sources are missing or stale; progress stays visible. After an analysis-service update, restart DeusEdix before refreshing so that the new backend is loaded.

Scanning never saves or Rebuilds maps, compiles runtime code, changes dialogue, writes flags or alters a running game. Generated maps need an existing built snapshot; missing coverage is reported instead of silently building one. Esc closes the window. Inspecting a rule does not apply any change.

Story

Story (top bar), or Dialogues... on a selected character, opens the story of the map. It has three lists: dialogues, InfoLinks and events. Save keeps your work; Apply in game turns it into UnrealScript, compiles it into DeusEdix's package and installs it (the game must be closed; it takes a few seconds).

Everything is checked before it is applied: an empty line, an answer that leads nowhere or a missing character are listed under the panel.

Dialogues as a graph, and Play-test

A dialogue opens as a Graph (the List button shows the same steps in order). Each step is a node: a line in the colour of who speaks (JC blue, the character violet), JC's answers with an arrow from each one, conditions with Yes and No; the arrows show where the dialogue goes on.

  • Drag the dot under a step onto another step to connect them, or onto empty space to add the next step there (a menu offers every kind). Double-click empty space for a step on its own.
  • Enter adds the next line, said by the other speaker, ready to write. Delete removes a step: what led to it goes on to where it went. Ctrl+Z undoes. Fit shows the whole dialogue, Tidy lays it out again.
  • The selected step is edited at the side, with the same fields as in the List. Steps nothing reaches are faded; a step with a problem (an empty line, an answer that goes nowhere) is marked.

Play-test plays the dialogue in the editor as the game will: choose JC's answers, and the step it is at lights up. At the side, the state it reads (flags, credits, health, skill points, what JC carries): change it and the dialogue starts again with it. Answers the game would hide are greyed.

Dialogues

A dialogue belongs to a character or an object. It starts when JC talks to them (a click) or when JC gets close (within a distance, and only if the character can see him), once or every time, and only if the flags under Only if have the given values. Steps play in order:

Step
Linethe speaker, JC or another character of the map says something
JC's answers2 to 6 answers; each one goes to its own label; an answer can show up only if a flag has a value
Labela point the answers and jumps go to
If a flag...jumps to a label if a flag has a value

Characters keep the conversation name they have in the original game, so their original dialogues keep working.

Priority. A character can have more than one dialogue (one if JC killed someone, one if that someone is alive, one for everything else). When more than one could start, the first from the top whose Only if conditions hold is the one that plays: put the most specific ones first and the general one last. ▲ ▼ next to Priority move a dialogue before or after the same character's other dialogues. A dialogue set to play once is skipped after it has played, and the next one plays.

Who is this? The lists of characters show the name in the map, what they are, their conversation name and the part of the map they are in (for example UCRoofGuard0 (Special Projects Security) · UCHKGuard — Roof access). The target button next to a list (and Show in scene next to the speaker) shows that character in the 3D view: the panel steps aside to a bar, the character is selected with its properties on the right; Back to the story (or Esc) brings the panel back as it was. The same button is next to the characters and doors of the actions and of the event a character dies.

The Story panel shows your dialogues and a separate Original conversations list. Dialogues written directly in UnrealScript are not listed.

Original conversations and Game voices

Open an original map, then Map > Story.... Original conversations lists its characters' dialogues and the mission's InfoLinks. Select a character in the list, or select one in the 3D view and open Dialogues.... The script shows how it starts, its flag conditions, speakers, answers, jumps and other events. Click ▶ beside a line to hear its original voice; click again to stop. Expand an event to inspect its details. Mission-wide InfoLinks are included because some are started by mission scripts, so a listed call is not guaranteed to happen at that exact location.

In the media browser, Game voices searches by a sentence, a character or a conversation name. Filter by Mission and Character, open a character and conversation, then listen to a line. Show more lines loads the next results. Voices chooses an installed package: Revision's normal JC, its female JC variant, or the original Deus Ex. The F packages are for female JC; they are not French translations. Text and voices come from your installed packages.

Choose Type > Ambient NPC phrases in Game voices to find barks and non-interactive reactions. The filter starts across all missions, then you can narrow it by character or mission, search a phrase and play it. These reactions are read-only; their voices can still be used by reference on your own Story lines.

Under Original conversations, Mission flags lists literal flag names used by the map's actual mission script and its parent classes. Search names or functions, expand a flag to see its operation, class, function and source line. Boolean flags also appear in Story suggestions with their origin; integer counters are listed for reference, not offered as boolean switches. These are references in the installed scripts, not live values from a save. Dynamic flag names are not guessed. If a package has no script source, the panel reports that limitation.

Original flags, goal names and notes also appear in the Story suggestions, with conversation names as their source. Characters with original conversations have a speech icon in the hierarchy. Their BindName is read only in the Inspector because the game uses it to attach conversations.

Replace a voice: use Record or Import... under an original line, or drop one of your sounds onto its voice bar. The original text and story logic stay intact. The same speaker saying the same text uses the same recording, including occurrences in other conversations. × removes that recording and restores the original voice. Close Revision and press Apply in game after adding or removing a dub. The game uses the recording with its normal speaking animation; lines without a dub keep their own original audio. Radio calls support replacement voices too.

Use a game voice: drag a line from Game voices onto one of your Story voice bars. You can also click Select voice in the browser, then Use game voice on your line. Only a reference is saved: the player's installed game supplies the audio. Changing the text or speaker clears this reference so an old recording does not silently speak the wrong words. Your own recordings take priority over references for the same speaker and text.

Edit a copy creates an editable dialogue with the original owner, activation rules, priority, conditions and voice references. It keeps the original conversation name, so mission-script calls and the game's “already played” flag still work. Edit its lines and steps, then Save and Apply in game. Credits, skill points, inventory checks, choice requirements, animations, random branches, trading events, notes and jumps into other conversations are preserved. The copy uses the editor's automatic cameras; a notice explains this. Existing missing or repeated labels are reported for review. An unsupported event blocks conversion and is listed instead of being dropped.

Switch off disables that conversation in this map. On an editable copy, the checkbox keeps its steps so you can turn it back on. Restore original removes the map's override; apply again and reopen the map in the game. Recordings for matching lines are separate: remove a dub from its voice bar if you want its original audio as well. After changing compiled stories, test from a fresh map start; saves made against an older version of your story can retain older state.

The original game packages stay unchanged. The catalogue and preview audio stay in the local editor/cache/_gamecons and editor/cache/_gamevoices folders; neither is part of the editor's repository or installer. Original voices are never imported into the editor's sound package.

InfoLinks

An InfoLink is a radio call. It is one-way: only the caller speaks; JC never answers by radio (if JC has to report something, set a flag). Choose the caller among the portraits of the game (Alex Jacobson, Walton Simons, Jock, Paul Denton, Sam Carter, Jaime Reyes, Anna Navarre, Gunther Hermann, Tracer Tong, Bob Page, Morgan Everett, Gary Savage, Stanton Dowd, Daedalus, Icarus) or someone else with any name. Each line gets its own screen, timed like the game's.

An InfoLink never starts over a conversation or another InfoLink: it waits. As in Deus Ex, its text goes to the history and its actions happen at the start, so an InfoLink cut short counts as heard. Events and dialogues play it (action Play an InfoLink); + Event that plays it creates the event for you, and Plays when lists what plays it.

No talking while it plays (on unless you turn it off): while the call is on screen, clicking people starts no dialogue, because a dialogue would hide the InfoLink window and the game would cut the call. As soon as it ends, people can be talked to again. Turn it off for a call that may play while JC is busy with someone.

Events

An event happens when JC gets close to a point, when a flag changes, at map start, when a character dies or is knocked out, when a faction turns hostile to JC, or when JC has an item, once or every time, and only if its conditions are met. For the point, select an object in the scene and press Use the selected object, or look at the spot and press Use the point in front of the camera.

Actions

Events, dialogues (step Actions) and InfoLinks can do:

Action

On the walkable area (under Walk / Run to a place, in the Interactions and in the Story; on by default for the ones you make now): the character finds the way on the walkable area (View > Walkable area), the way a man would walk, and needs no path nodes on the way. Each Rebuild of a map that uses it puts the walkable area into the map (Walkable area for the game: N polygons in Messages). In the game the mod plans the route (the corners where it turns) and the character walks it with the game's own orders, so it opens the doors it can open and animates as always. If it gets held up, the mod plans the route again from where it stands. Without a walkable area in the map, or with no way on it, the character takes the AI paths as before. What it knows that the view does not:

  • crates, barricades and the other blocking objects (JC climbs over them and squeezes past them; characters cannot), and the invisible walls for characters (BlockAll);
  • locked doors, which are walls for characters. A Battery Park trooper cannot get into Castle Clinton: its two doors are locked.

Old actions (without the tick) and the characters of the original maps keep the AI paths. For Tools > Scripts: Map settings > walkable area in the game puts it into the map even with no action using it; then class'UCNavRoute'.static.Send(somePawn, aPlace, bRun) sends a character, and class'UCNavMesh'.static.Of(self).Route(a, b) plans a route (NumCorners, CornerAt(i)).

Flags

Flags are the story's memory: one dialogue sets them, another requires them. The flag field suggests the flags already in use, plus two kinds the story makes by itself: UCEd<code>_<id>_Played (a dialogue has been played) and UCEd<code>_<id>_Heard (an InfoLink has been heard); the InfoLink panel shows its own.

What happened to a character. DeusEdix remembers, for the whole game, every character JC killed or knocked out, in every map: no flag to set by hand. The 👤 button next to every flag field (Only if, If a flag..., events, Set a flag) picks it: the map (this one or any other), the character and was killed, was knocked out, was killed or knocked out or is alive. The field then reads, for example, 👤 TriadRedArrow0 (06_HongKong_WanChai_Street) was killed. Example: two dialogues for Simons in a later map, the first Only if that Red Arrow member was killed, the second with no condition: Simons brings it up only if JC killed him.

They are ordinary flags (UCFate<map code>_<name>_Dead, _KO, _Down), so a story can also set them itself with Set a flag: a character who "dies" in a cutscene counts as killed. The last value written wins. A knocked-out character that is killed later counts as killed; one carried to another spot keeps its fate.

Example: a guard gives JC a weapon, then a radio call

  1. Select the guard in the scene and press Dialogues...: a new dialogue opens with the guard as the speaker. Set Starts to when JC gets close.
  2. Write his line. Add JC's answers with three answers (GEP gun, crossbow, sniper rifle).
  3. Each answer created its own branch at the bottom: a label (answer1, answer2...), a line and End of dialogue. In each branch add Give JC an item with that weapon and move it up (↑) under the label.
  4. In each branch, before End of dialogue, add Actions with A character... (the guard) walks to a point, and pick the point.
  5. Create an InfoLink: caller Alex Jacobson, one or two lines.
  6. In the guard's dialogue, add to the Actions step Play an InfoLink (Alex's).
  7. Apply in game, open the game and walk to the guard.

When something does not start

The game's log (Revision\System\Revision.log) explains it, in lines starting with UCEd:: why a "when JC gets close" dialogue did not start (JC out of sight, its conditions, the game refusing it), where a character was sent, which door was used, which InfoLink played.

Voices: dubbing the lines

Every line of a dialogue or an InfoLink can have its own voice, like the game's recorded lines:

  • ● Record under the line: the window shows the line to read (as in a dubbing room); Record, speak, Stop, ▶ Listen, then Use this take (or Again). The silence before and after is cut, a quiet take is raised to the level of the game's voices. ⚙ next to Record (or Settings > Microphone) chooses the microphone.
  • Import... takes a recorded file (WAV, MP3, OGG, FLAC); a file from Windows or one of My sounds can also be dropped on the line.
  • ▶ listens to it, ✕ takes it away.

In the game the character says it from their mouth, and the lips follow: as in Deus Ex, there is nothing to prepare for the lips, the engine moves the mouth (seven shapes: A, E, F, M, O, T, U and closed) with the sound of the line while it plays. An InfoLink's voice comes over the radio, with the portrait. The voice belongs to the line: change its text and the voice follows it when you Save. It plays after Apply in game. Voices are kept in the editor's sounds package (UCSounds in DeusEdix, DeusEdixSounds in DeusEdix), installed with your maps. A line too long for one subtitle (over about 250 letters) is shown in two parts: split it into two lines to give each its voice.

When you install a project, DeusEdix puts its maps in a folder of its own inside the game (DeusEdixProjects) and adds it to the game's settings (Revision.ini, or DeusEx.ini for the original game), so the game reads your maps before its own (a backup is kept: Revision.ini.bak_projects). The installer also puts DeusEdixNav.u and DeusEdixNav.dll into each game's System folder: the characters of DeusEdix maps and mods use them to find their way.

To uninstall DeusEdix, use Start > Uninstall DeusEdix, Windows' installed apps, or run the installer again and choose Uninstall. It always takes DeusEdix's lines out of the games' settings and removes the workspace and the caches. It also asks whether to remove everything else DeusEdix put into the games (the maps, saves and mods it installed, and DeusEdixNav, which mods made with DeusEdix need); that is kept unless you tick it. Your projects are never removed: they stay in the Projects folder for a later install.

Local geometry (Experimental)

Enable Settings > Editing > Local geometry (Experimental), then choose Map > Local geometry (Experimental) or the same command in the Rebuild menu. The tool opens a separate window with a private copy of the map currently shown. Rebuild normally first if you want to include saved editor changes; those changes are not applied automatically inside this tool. The temporary tutorial does not use it.

Select a source brush and enter its movement, scale or rotation. These transforms use that brush's pivot. Before / After show the compiled geometry after verification. The default Area · Full check reconstructs the affected area and still uses a Full rebuild to check the result. Isolated box, Convex CSG and Buried detail offer independent verification without Full only for supported geometry. Unsupported changes are refused; a successful edit on one brush does not imply that every edit on the map is supported.

It works the same for Deus Ex: Revision and the original Deus Ex. What the modes without Full accept: Isolated box, a plain box floating inside one room, on a map of at most 24 plain boxes; Convex CSG, a map of at most 24 plain convex brushes, lit or not, with one room and no portals, and an edit whose area leaves out at least one other brush; Buried detail, one detail (semisolid) brush that only grows inside solid. On a lit map the faces a result rebuilt have no light until you rebuild Lighting.

Results are experimental copies outside the normal project build. Show result opens their folder; Continue from result starts another edit from the verified copy. To keep one in your project, use Open map > Browse .dx to import it as a new map. Rebuild its Lighting and Paths before Play or Install. The tool does not replace the project's map or install a result automatically. Verification can take a few minutes and this preview is not a speed guarantee.

The feature uses the engine from your installed game. No game maps, packages, textures or engine DLLs are included in DeusEdix.

Limits

  • Some original maps cannot be rebuilt (Wan Chai Street): only objects can change there.
  • A map's BSP has a limit (65536 nodes): Rebuild optimises it as UnrealEd did, so the big maps of Revision stay under it; a map that still goes over says so in Messages (fewer or simpler blocks there).
  • Stretching a structure of an existing map needs a rebuild of its geometry, so it works only where the map can be rebuilt.
  • The Animation Studio works on human characters (the skeleton has hips, spine, head, arms and legs): animals and robots can play and use the game's animations, not new ones. The fingers and the face do not move (Deus Ex has no bones for them: the mouth is the game's lip sync).
  • New animations are as smooth as the frames of the model allow: a character with few vertices (the original models) bends at the joints like the game's own animations do.
  • Original maps that cannot be rebuilt (Wan Chai Street) cannot get Pieces either (they need a rebuild of the geometry); a copy of such a map has the same limit.
  • Characters of the Creator have no barks (the short lines the game's characters say by themselves) and keep the bodies of the game: new 3D models cannot be imported.
  • The editor does not make voices by itself: record or import them for each line (see Voices).
  • The Lit view is close to the game but not identical: lamps that flicker or pulse are shown steady, coronas and fog are not drawn, and some objects can have a different skin in the game.
  • Animations move objects, not characters (their AI walks them: give them orders and patrols). The keys of a door or an elevator are the engine's: at most 8, and the same curve for all of them.
  • Imported models do not move by themselves (no skeletal animations: characters are made with the Character Creator), and their collision is a cylinder round them (untick Solid for a long table JC should reach over). A map that uses them needs DeusEdixModels.u (Install puts it in the game).
  • A zone works only when its area is closed: walls and zone portals all round. Water and portals change the geometry: they need a map that can be rebuilt.
  • Interactions, stories, cutscenes and the editor's characters run through DeusEdix's package (DeusEdix.u, which Install puts in the game): a map that uses them needs it. Security cameras give no event when they see JC (the game has none): use a zone instead.
  • A door opened by an interaction or a Story action behaves as when JC opens it (it triggers its Event when it has opened): a door that shuts by itself after some seconds (stays open) shuts again.
